# Value analysis — what this is actually worth spending

Written 2026-08-12, after the traveller profile was captured. Read
[`../context/travel-profile.md`](../context/travel-profile.md) first; the usage
pattern drives everything below.

Prices are US retail, pre-tax, verified 2026-08-12 unless marked as a band.

## The correction that reshapes the plan

The buyer's working assumption was "a fairly affordable 15.6″ monitor plus a
desktop or VESA stand".

**There is no such thing as an affordable 15.6″ desktop monitor.** The category
does not exist. True 15″ desktop panels were phased out around 2018, and
everything now sold in the 14–16″ range is a **portable monitor** — kickstand,
USB-C, travel case, priced accordingly. The practical floor for a conventional
desktop monitor with its own stand is **19″**, and 19″ panels are mostly 1600×900
or 1440×900 at **$85–120** — more money than a 24″ 1080p and a worse screen.

The economics behind this are structural, not a passing gap in the market: the
chassis, stand, PSU and controller board for a small panel cost about the same as
for a 24″ one, so nobody builds them.

So "a cheap 15.6″ monitor" resolves, unavoidably, to **buying a portable monitor** —
the exact product category the buyer has bought several times and found
underwhelming — and paying its portability premium for a screen that will never
leave the room. That is the one outcome the analysis should prevent.

## Cost per day of actual use

The right denominator here is not years of ownership but **working days at the
destination**: roughly 15 per year (three weeks, one visit).

| Option | Price | $/day @ 5 yr (75 days) | $/day @ 10 yr (150 days) | Screen area |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Used 24″ 1080p, local pickup | ~$30 | **$0.40** | $0.20 | 247 in² |
| Refurb Dell P2419H-class 24″, ergo stand included | ~$80 | **$1.07** | $0.53 | 247 in² |
| New Dell SE2426H 24″ + MOUNTUP stand | $99 | **$1.32** | $0.66 | 247 in² |
| **15.6″ portable + VESA stand** | **$170** | **$2.27** | $1.13 | **104 in²** |
| New 27″ 1440p, full ergo stand | $200 | $2.67 | $1.33 | 311 in² |
| 27″ 4K USB-C | ~$290 | $3.87 | $1.93 | 311 in² |

**The 15.6″ portable route costs roughly what a 27″ 1440p costs per day of use, and
delivers one-third of the screen.** It is dominated by every desktop option in the
table — more expensive than three of them and smaller than all five. There is no
scenario in this profile where it is the value answer.

## Where the money actually goes

The entire decision spans about **$30 to $290**. Over 15 working days a year, the
gap between the cheapest sensible option and the most expensive one is **$1–3 per
day of use**. Set against three weeks of not working hunched over a laptop panel,
that spread is noise.

The conclusion that follows is not "buy the cheapest" and not "buy the best" — it
is that **price optimisation is the wrong objective here, because the amounts are
too small to reward it**. What *is* worth optimising is:

1. **Not paying for portability**, since the premium is real (2–4× per in²) and buys
   a property with zero value in this profile.
2. **Getting eye-level height**, which is the stated reason for the purchase and is
   free if the monitor's own stand provides it.
3. **Keeping the abandonment loss small**, because if the visits stop, whatever was
   bought is stranded in Connecticut.

Points 1 and 3 both push down-market; point 2 sets a floor. They meet at a
**refurbished business monitor**.

## The value answer: refurbished business-class

A Dell P-series or UltraSharp is the specific thing to look for, because the
business lines ship the **full ergonomic stand — height, tilt, swivel, pivot —
as standard**, plus VESA 100×100 and a proper port selection. That makes the mount
free, which removes the $23–29 stand from the bill entirely.

Budget/consumer lines (Dell E-series, S-series) are tilt-only and need the separate
stand, so they are not automatically cheaper once the mount is counted.

$30 monitor that turns out to be mediocre is not a mistake worth mourning.

**Check first, spend nothing:** households frequently have a disused monitor or an
unused TV in a spare room. At 1080p over HDMI either works fine as a second screen
on this laptop. Free beats every row in the table above, and costs one question.

## Settled 2026-08-12

Three answers came back from the buyer and close the remaining questions:

- **No spare monitor at the property.** The free option is out.
- **There is somewhere to store it.** The small-panel route, which only ever
  survived on the storage constraint, is now closed for good.
- **24″, not 21″.** The buyer's long-standing size is 21″, but that is in a
  *three-monitor* desktop setup. Here a single panel carries the whole workload
  alongside the laptop screen, so the extra area is worth having. This also lands
  where the value is: 24″ refurbished business stock is the deepest, cheapest and
  best-supported segment of the used market.

## Buying decision: new, firsthand

**Stated by the buyer 2026-08-12 and it overrides the pure value ranking:** buy
new. The reasoning is start from a known-good unit rather than trust someone
else's account of an item's condition. That is a legitimate call and the analysis
does not fight it — at 15 days of use a year the spread between $67 refurbished
and $240 new is about **$2.30 per day of use**, which is exactly the kind of
difference this document has argued throughout is too small to optimise.

What buying new actually purchases here, beyond peace of mind:

- **A real warranty.** Dell P-series carries **3 years Basic Hardware Service with
  Advanced Exchange**. Against refurbished terms of 30–90 days, this is the one
  place the money buys something concrete — and it matters more than usual for a
  device sitting unattended in another country for eleven months at a stretch.
- **No grading lottery.** Refurb "Grade C" is cosmetic-only in theory; in practice
  it is a stranger's judgement, and a return from Connecticut is not convenient.
- **Current models**, so a replacement or a second unit is still purchasable in a
  few years.

## Recommendation: Dell P2425, $239.99

The 16:10 panel is the reason. **1920×1200 is 11% more vertical space than
1920×1080** — and vertical is the axis that matters for terminals, documents and
long-form reading, which is what this machine does. It is also the only partial
compensation available for dropping from three screens to one. The premium over
the 16:9 P2425H is $55, or **$0.73 per day of use over five years**.

Confirmed stand specs (Dell spec page and the P2225H/P2425H/P2425 user's guide):

| | |
|---|---|
| Height | **150 mm** (5.9″) |
| Tilt | −5° / +21° |
| Swivel | −45° / +45° |
| Pivot | −90° / +90°, either direction |
| VESA | 100×100 mm, quick-release |
| Panel | 24.07″ IPS, 1920×1200, 100 Hz, 300 nits, 99% sRGB, 1500:1 |
| Ports | HDMI 1.4, DP 1.2, VGA, USB hub (USB-C downstream, data only, 15 W) |

**Watch the model naming — it is a genuine trap.** In this family:

- `P2425H` = 16:9, 1920×1080
- `P2425` = **16:10, 1920×1200** ← the one to buy
- `P2425E` = 16:10 plus USB-C 90 W PD and RJ45 Ethernet — a different, pricier SKU

Read the **resolution field**, not the model number, at the point of purchase.

### Buy a cable — one is not in the box

The P2425 ships with a **DisplayPort-to-DisplayPort** cable and a USB A-to-B
cable. **The ThinkPad E15 Gen 3 has no DisplayPort socket**, so the included video
cable is useless on this laptop. Either:

- **HDMI cable** (~$8) → 1920×1200 @ 60 Hz. Simplest, and fine.
- **USB-C-to-DisplayPort cable** (~$15–20) → 1920×1200 @ 100 Hz, using the laptop's
  DisplayPort 1.2 Alt Mode. Worth the extra few dollars to get the panel's full
  refresh rate.

The monitor's USB-C port is **downstream and data-only at 15 W** — it does not
charge the laptop and is not a video input. The laptop's own charger still travels.

### If a cheaper new option is wanted

[HP E24 G5 at $99](https://www.walmart.com/ip/HP-E24-G5-FHD-Monitor-23-8-FHD-1920-x-1080-50-75-Hz/2144125539) —
same four-way business stand, 23.8″ 1080p IPS, 99% sRGB. The caveat is that HP has
declared it **end-of-life**, so current stock is clearance and long-term warranty
support is limited. For a screen used 15 days a year that matters less than it
would in a fleet, but it is the opposite of the "known-good, buy once" instinct
that drove the decision to buy new in the first place.

## On the second location

If the European visit ever becomes a regular working stay, **buy a second cheap
monitor there** rather than shuttling one. Two local refurbs at ~$80 each cost less
than one portable ($170) plus a hard case (~$50), carry no transit risk, and avoid
the failure mode that already destroyed one monitor in checked luggage. The
"scattered monitors" instinct the buyer was uneasy about is, at these prices,
straightforwardly correct — it is cheaper than transport, not more expensive.
